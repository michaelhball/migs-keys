## 4/4 and 3/8: what changes?

The two numbers describe how written note lengths fit into each bar. The upper number counts units; the lower number identifies the unit.

| Time signature | Units in one bar | Count slowly | Divide each unit in half |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4/4 | Four quarter notes | 1 2 3 4 | 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & — eight eighth notes |
| 3/8 | Three eighth notes | 1 2 3 | 1 & 2 & 3 & — six sixteenth notes |

A quarter note lasts as long as two eighth notes. An eighth note lasts as long as two sixteenth notes. Those relationships stay the same in both meters. **The time signature does not specify speed:** tempo does that.

In 4/4, feel four beats, with beat 1 starting each bar and often a smaller emphasis on beat 3. In slow 3/8 practice, feel three eighth-note pulses, with beat 1 beginning each group. At a flowing performance tempo, a musician may feel the whole 3/8 bar as one larger beat, divided into three. That larger beat is a dotted quarter note.

The earlier 4/4 version was a separate pitch exercise. Changing its time-signature label would not turn it into Beethoven's rhythm: we also need different note lengths and bar placements. The player now uses the original rhythm.

## Count the actual opening

The first E–D♯ pair is a **pickup**: an incomplete bar leading into the first full bar. Each is a sixteenth note. Count them **3 &**, then start **1 & 2 & 3 &** for the following six notes.

The first A arrives on **1 of the next bar**. It is an eighth note, so it lasts for **1 &** — twice as long as each of the preceding sixteenth notes. The left hand enters on that same 1.

Before playing, tap three evenly spaced pulses and say “1 & 2 & 3 &”. Your taps land on the numbers; the “&” falls halfway between taps. Then count one empty bar and come in on 3 & of the next. Keep counting when either hand rests.

## Tempo in this player

The control says **eighth notes / minute**. At 60, each numbered count lasts one second and each sixteenth note lasts half a second. A complete 3/8 bar lasts three seconds. At 120, those durations halve.

That is different from the early 4/4 lessons, whose BPM control counts quarter notes. **Quarter = 60 and eighth = 120 give eighth notes the same duration.** Always check which note value a tempo marking counts. The number alone is incomplete.

## Practise the opening with both hands

1. Choose the opening passage and **Right hand**. Try 60–90 eighth notes per minute. Find E5 above C5; D♯5 is the black key immediately to its left. Learn the pickup and first complete bar before continuing.
2. Stop on the first A. Practise the preceding four notes into that A so you can cross the bar line without hesitating. Count aloud rather than relying only on how the tune sounds in your head.
3. Choose **Left hand**. It rests through the pickup and first full bar, then plays A2–E3–A3. Use 5–2–1 as a starting fingering. Allow the hand to move; avoid holding an uncomfortable stretch.
4. Choose **Both hands** and practise the first A together with the left-hand A2. Add the next left-hand notes, then the answering right-hand notes.
5. Extend by one bar. Practise the new bar alone, then the join: the last part of the previous bar into the new bar. Repeat the joined section slowly before running the whole passage.

Use the passage selector to explore the complete piece. Later passages overlap by a bar so you can practise joins. Their numbers count through the performance, including repeats; after repeats they differ from the printed score. The later contrasting sections are much harder than the familiar opening and can remain a longer-term project.

## Markings and theory review

**Poco moto** means “with a little motion”: a gently moving character, not an exact BPM. **pp** means *pianissimo*, very soft. A dot after a note adds half its original value; a dotted quarter therefore lasts three eighth notes. The little flags or beams identify eighth notes (one) and sixteenth notes (two).
